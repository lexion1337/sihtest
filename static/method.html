The decision rule

One procedure decides whether a change is reported as supported. It is declared once in the code — analysis.PRIMARY_RULE — so it cannot be chosen per finding after the fact.

Loading...

Why declare it in one place: reporting two tests without saying which one decides invites picking whichever supports each finding. verify.py asserts that the supported list is a strict consequence of this rule, so a drifting implementation fails loudly instead of quietly.

Frozen is not the same as calibrated.

Fixing one procedure stops us picking whichever test flatters a finding. It does not show the p-values behave well. Benjamini-Hochberg cannot repair poorly calibrated inputs, and at these sample sizes — with expected cell counts of one to three — the normal approximation is exactly where calibration is doubtful. What would settle it: measuring this procedure's empirical false-discovery rate and power across repeated simulations with known ground truth, or adopting a prespecified exact procedure and recomputing the whole family. Neither is done. Treat these p-values as reproducible, not as validated.

The Fisher cross-check, and what it does not do

The primary test is a pooled two-proportion z-test — a normal approximation. At these sample sizes, a competency near 5% prevalence produces expected cell counts of one to three, which is where that approximation is weakest. So a two-sided Fisher exact test is computed for every competency and displayed alongside.

It gates nothing.

Fisher is reported as a conservative cross-check, not as a second chance for a finding that failed the primary rule. If a competency passes Fisher and fails the declared rule, it is not reported as supported. Showing a second test that could overturn the first would be exactly the freedom the declared rule exists to remove.

Confidence intervals

Two different intervals are used, for two different quantities, and they are never swapped:

  • Wilson score intervals for a prevalence — "what share of job ads mentioned this". Wilson rather than the textbook normal interval because the normal one misbehaves badly near 0% and 100%, which is where several of these competencies sit.
  • Newcombe intervals for the difference between two prevalences — "how much it changed". A difference needs its own interval; reading it off two overlapping prevalence intervals is a known error that systematically understates the evidence.

Does the answer depend on where we drew the line?

Two thresholds are judgement calls: how prevalent a competency must be, and how large a change must be, before it is worth an administrator's attention. Both are re-run across a grid so a reader can see which recommendations survive a different call.

Two accuracy audits, reported unflatteringly

Every number in this project rests on two mechanical steps: deciding which role a job ad is for, and deciding which skills it mentions. Both were hand-checked against sampled postings. Neither result is good, and both are published as they came out.

The strongest objection to both audits.

The same author wrote the classifier, the dictionary, and the verdicts these scores are computed against — so the audits share their blind spots, and terms the dictionary never contained are exactly the ones its author is least likely to notice missing. Treat the missed lists as a lower bound, and both audits as a machine first pass pending independent human confirmation. Sixteen and six postings are small samples.

Until that review is done, this funnel's endpoint overstates usefulness.

Adding more sources did not fix this.

Going from two boards to four raised the postings collected by 48 and the historically eligible count by one — the yield percentage fell. The constraint is not how many boards we query; it is how few collected postings are both in a measured role and carry a true creation date.

Numerical invariants

python verify.py asserts 67 numerical invariants across seven groups, and exits non-zero if any fails. It is not a test suite; it protects the one claim the whole project rests on — that every number on screen traces to records in the database.

  • Every reported share equals its own numerator over its own denominator.
  • Every denominator equals the postings actually in that role-and-quarter bucket — not the role total, not the corpus total.
  • Every bucket below the threshold carries the low-confidence flag.
  • The supported list is exactly what the declared rule produces — no row can be supported on a raw p-value, and none on Fisher.
  • Records excluded from the time series are excluded from every quarterly denominator, and counted in the exclusion total.
  • Provenance counts add up to the corpus, and are derived from the records rather than asserted.
  • A "never seen before" claim carries the denominator of the whole window, not of the latest quarter.

That last one was a real defect this file caught: a never-seen claim was reporting the latest quarter's sample size for a statement spanning the entire window, understating the evidence roughly eightfold.

Limits, stated in full
  • Calibration is not demonstrated. The procedure is frozen and reproducible. Its empirical false-discovery rate and power have not been measured. This is the weakest statistical claim in the project.
  • Non-identification. We can show a competency is in demand and untaught. We cannot show that a trainee from a given district could be hired into those jobs — advertised location is not accessibility. This is the strongest argument against the project, and it is unresolved.
  • Coverage skew. Real postings come from a handful of company ATS boards that skew heavily toward funded technology companies. They are not a probability sample of Indian employment, and nothing here should be read as a national estimate.
  • Closed vocabulary. Extraction runs against a fixed dictionary, so the honest claim is "changing prevalence of tracked competencies", never the discovery of genuinely new skills. Open-vocabulary scanning is run separately as a discovery aid and is counted nowhere.
  • Independence. Benjamini-Hochberg assumes valid null p-values and a dependence structure it tolerates. Competencies co-occur within a posting and the same employers recur across periods, so that assumption is not established here. Correcting is an improvement over raw p-values, not a warranty.
  • Per-role family. The correction family is every competency measured for one role. It does not control a list pooled across roles, districts, or repeated releases. If those are ever combined, the family must be redeclared.
  • The syllabus is a stand-in. No NSQF qualification pack or ITI curriculum has been mapped yet, so no finding here is yet tied to a real qualification.
  • Mention is not requirement. A skill named in company boilerplate is counted the same as one in the requirements section. That inflates every share by an unknown amount.

Also worth stating plainly: about "two false alerts in forty tests at p<0.05" — that describes what would happen if all forty nulls were true and each test were properly calibrated. It is not an estimate of how many of our findings are false.
