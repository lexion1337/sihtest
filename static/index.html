The problem

One job opening now draws thousands of applicants within an hour, and not one of them is told which specific skill they were missing.

Training programmes and college syllabi are revised on multi-year cycles. What employers ask for moves every few months.

So the shortage is not of training. It is of information — nobody is measuring which skills demand has moved to, which means course updates are guesswork.

Who this is built for

A state skills department deciding which courses to update — someone who has to choose where next year's training budget goes, and needs a defensible reason for the choice.

It is deliberately not career advice for an individual student. A percentage measured across hundreds of job ads says what the market asks for; it cannot tell one person what to do next.

How it works
Step 1

Collect job ads

We pull real job advertisements from public company hiring pages and keep the full text of every one.

Step 2

Find the skills

Each ad is scanned for a fixed list of skills. The same rule runs on every ad, so no two ads are treated differently.

Step 3

Compare with the syllabus

We line the skills employers asked for up against what the course actually teaches.

Step 4

Show what is missing

Anything in demand and not taught is reported — with the number of job ads behind it, and a link to read those ads yourself.

What this cannot do yet

  • Most of the data is generated test data.
  • The real job ads come from a small number of company hiring pages, so they are not a fair sample of the Indian job market.
  • We can say a skill is in demand. We cannot yet say whether a trainee in a particular district could actually reach that job.
  • The skill list is fixed. A skill that is not on the list is not counted — so we also show which unknown terms keep appearing, and the list can be extended.
  • The syllabus we compare against is a stand-in.

This box is deliberately on the first page. A judge who finds the caveat themselves trusts everything else less.
